# YouTube research protocol

## Purpose and boundaries

Research informs editorial judgment. It does not produce a calibrated chance of reaching a view target. Use read-only public research and explicitly authorized first-party analytics. Do not buy access, scrape around access controls, contact creators, download their work for reuse, or fabricate missing information.

Search results are leads. Open the video/channel or inspect an authorized metadata result where possible. If an indexed snippet is the only available observation, retain it as discovery/context evidence unless the metric's actual observation provenance is reliable and explicit. A publication date or page crawl date does not establish when the displayed view count was measured. Do not use the time you opened an old snippet as a fresh metric-observation timestamp. A transcript proves access to its text, not that the video was watched or audio auditioned.

## Sampling

1. Read the candidate's audience, language, format, runtime and research mode.
2. Search the specific event, the closest story category, the audience's likely question and the relevant visual/storytelling format. Log queries, timestamps and why they were chosen.
3. Collect 6–12 useful comparables. Approval requires six unique eligible videos from at least three distinct channels. Shorts URLs and watch URLs for the same video count once.
4. For `recent_trend`, counted videos must be published within 90 days, including at least two within 30 days. For `evergreen`, use 180 days, including at least two within 90 days. Evergreen findings must not claim that the exact event is trending.
5. Include at least two ordinary recent uploads selected by recency/relevance, not because they performed exceptionally. Record exclusions and selection bias in limitations.
6. Use the same language, comparable duration and intended audience. The numeric sample accepts durations from half the candidate's minimum through 1.5 times its maximum (20–67.5 seconds for a 40–45-second candidate). This is a project comparability rule. Explain format match. A substantially different format can be contextual evidence but cannot silently substitute for a comparable Short.
7. Inspect at least two relevant transcripts/videos before making specific creative comparisons. Metadata-only research can establish observed interest, but cannot support claims about an unseen hook, edit or ending.

Do not silently change mode, audience or inclusion criteria after seeing disappointing results. Broaden a sparse query transparently. If the minimum cannot be met, return BLOCKED, preserve useful findings, and specify the additional evidence or candidate change needed.

## Record observations

Each comparison records its unique video ID, URL, stable channel identifier or canonical handle, title, publication date, observation timestamp, duration, observed public views, language, format match, selection method, inspection level, evidence location, relevance and limits. Record exact observed values when possible. Do not turn an abbreviated display such as “1.2M” into a claimed exact count; note rounding and use a conservative approximate count explicitly described as such. Private metrics remain unknown unless the channel owner supplied them.

The seven-day observation freshness limit is a project rule. All comparison observations included in an APPROVE report must be refreshed within that window, even if six other fresh rows already satisfy coverage. Retain old research in the project history rather than presenting it as current observation. An old article may be a valid factual source, but its popularity at publication is not evidence of a current YouTube trend. Recheck time-sensitive policies when relevant instead of relying on this package's creation date.

## Interpretation

- Compare age cohorts and similar durations. Do not compare one upload's first day with another's lifetime total.
- Where available, collect a same-channel reference from ordinary eligible uploads. Explain the sample and missing context; avoid a channel-size normalization that invents subscriber counts at upload time.
- Public views are not qualifying/engaged views. They cannot reveal retention, swipe rate, watch satisfaction, conversion or revenue.
- `public_views / elapsed_days` is lifetime-average exposure. It is not measured current velocity. Two snapshots of the same metric at known times are needed to calculate a change; several independent observations are needed before describing a broad rising pattern.
- Strong results on established channels do not establish the probability of a new channel succeeding.
- A popular exact story may already be saturated. Identify what this episode newly explains or visualizes without copying wording, assets or sequence.
- Comments can reveal questions or confusion. They are a selected sample, not representative survey data, and are not factual evidence for the story itself.
- Google Trends or another search-interest source must name its product, query, geography and date range. Search interest is not YouTube video views.

## Findings to produce

State the audience-interest hypothesis, the observations supporting it, counterevidence, the candidate's distinctive contribution, the strongest failure risks and the confidence in this evidence packet. Distinguish observation, inference and recommendation. Keep numerical success probability and qualified-view forecast null.

## Learning after publication

When the owner supplies analytics, compare the reviewed predictions and actual results at matched ages such as 48 hours and seven days. Record engaged views, stayed-to-watch percentage, retention, subscribers gained and production effort where available. Use medians alongside standout results. Treat a relationship as a hypothesis until it recurs; differently distributed uploads are not a randomized A/B test.

Do not change scoring rules secretly after a disappointing result. Version revisions to this rubric and preserve the original review so the project can learn which judgments were useful.
