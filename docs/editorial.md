# Editorial system

These are StoryForge's production heuristics, not promises of retention or audience growth. Adapt them to the niche, audience, length and real channel evidence.

## Intake that becomes a reusable channel profile

Ask for the niche, approximate runtime, format and references before researching a new production. Also establish the audience, language, factual or fictional framing, voice preference, allowable services, and budget. Collect consequential missing information in one concise exchange. Reuse a saved brief instead of repeating questions already answered.

References can specify separate aspects: one video for pacing, one image for character proportions, a font sample for typography, and a soundtrack for mood. Record what each reference should influence and what should be avoided. A user saying “no references” is a meaningful answer: propose a provisional style and make the initial visual proof easy to review.

A useful channel profile retains the hook mode, narrator identity, character construction rules, palette, font, music approach, caption behavior, preferred topics, excluded themes, and prior episode IDs. Channel preferences are defaults; a new episode can override them explicitly. Do not assume the assets from an old project are approved for every new topic.

## Three distinct story types

**Documentary:** Maintain claim-level sources. Treat reported testimony as reported testimony, not proof of an interpretation. Search for the current resolution before calling a mystery unsolved. Do not invent quotes, dates, victims, motives, locations, official conclusions, or numbers for drama.

**Fiction:** Create original stories with a clear fictional framing. Maintain an internal continuity ledger for events, objects, characters and rules of the world. Research may inform settings and audience viability, but fictional events do not become historical facts through a realistic voice or visual treatment.

**Commentary:** Distinguish the documented event from analysis or opinion. Attribute disputed claims and explain what is supported. For conspiracy-related subjects, a compelling angle is often how a belief arose, what evidence exists, and what explanations fit—not an unsupported accusation presented as fact.

## Topic selection

Develop at least three candidates. Each needs a one-sentence angle, the central curiosity, a credible way to answer it, initial sources, an opening image, and the most expensive visual requirement. Research and production feasibility should influence the selection together. A story requiring footage or a complex simulation unavailable to the workflow needs a different treatment or a different candidate.

Select the topic because it has a specific payoff and a clear audience fit. “Strange things you never knew” is a category, not a story. The audience analyst's score helps compare candidates; the producer must still explain why this particular episode deserves to exist.

## Hook modes

| Mode | What stays consistent | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed | Exact approved prefix | The specific claim, image, stakes and payoff that immediately follow |
| Family | Rhetorical pattern and vocal rhythm | The opening sentence and story-specific detail |
| Topic | Channel tone and quality standard | The entire opening construction |

Write three candidate hooks. Choose one that makes a truthful promise, works aloud, and has an immediate visual. The first shot should reveal something rather than spend the whole hook on branding. A fixed phrase that consumes too much of a short film can be tightened only with the user's agreement; do not silently change an explicitly fixed prefix.

Possible hook families include a concrete contradiction, an overlooked detail, a surprising measured fact, and a question with a supported answer. Avoid unsupported absolutes such as “nobody knows,” “scientists cannot explain,” or “the government hid this” unless the exact claim is established. Avoid a recurring generic sentence that contains no story-specific curiosity.

## Short-form structure

For a roughly 45-second story, a useful first draft has an immediate hook, orientation by around the first fifth, escalation through the middle, a reveal or resolution near the last quarter, and a short closing beat. This is a pacing guide, not a fixed timeline shared by every film.

One setup and one satisfying payoff often work better than cramming five unrelated facts into the same runtime. Every visual beat should introduce evidence, movement, scale, consequence, or a new question. Do not cut to a new card merely because a timer expired.

Draft spoken English with short clauses and concrete nouns. Around 130–160 spoken words per minute can be a useful starting estimate for this style; actual performance, pauses, pronunciation and emphasis determine the final timing. The code's broader 70–220 WPM gate catches obviously implausible budgets, not good delivery. Never claim text-derived duration is a measured recording.

## Long-form structure

Long-form requires an argument or evolving story with chapters. Define the main question, the answer the viewer will eventually earn, and what each chapter contributes. Each chapter should contain new information or a changed interpretation, not a restated hook.

Plan a concise opening promise, enough orientation to understand the stakes, chapter-specific questions, evidence and explanations, intermediate payoffs, and a final synthesis. Track unresolved questions explicitly and close them honestly. Where the evidence remains uncertain, the payoff can be a clear account of what is known and why the rest remains unsettled.

Keep a section table with narration word target, evidence, visual treatment, scene count, intended emotion, narration take ID and actual measured duration. A director should plan both high-intensity and quiet moments. An editor should remove repetition before accelerating a voice recording.

## Script review

The independent fact checker receives the claim ledger and full script, including the hook, on-screen titles, metadata ideas and planned reveal. Verify every factual sentence and reject invented implication as well as invented wording. A technically literal line can still mislead if crucial context is omitted.

The editor checks whether each sentence earns its time. Replace vague adjectives with details; remove duplicated setup; pronounce names and numbers clearly; make transitions audible. The final line should complete the promise without forcing a repeated call to action.

## Originality and continuity

Compare the new episode with recent productions at the level of topic, script, visual events, explanation, conclusion, opening construction and asset use. Reuse visual components and characters as a design system, while authoring the substantive content for each episode. The policy review separately consults current YouTube guidance; see [policy and rights](policy-and-rights.md).

Track characters by ID with stable body proportions, costume, material colors, accessories, scale, and silhouette. Track story objects by ID with their location and state at each shot. A recurring object appearing in a different hand or a door opening in the wrong direction is an editing problem even if each individual frame looks attractive.

## Learning from released work

The post-publication analyst uses the user's authorized analytics export, labels its date range and metric definitions, and compares like formats with like formats. Record one or two testable production changes for the next batch, such as a clearer opening image or an earlier explanation. Do not treat a single successful episode as proof of a universal formula.
